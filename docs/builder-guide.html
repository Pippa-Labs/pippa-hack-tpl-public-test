Pippa Hackathon

Build an app for Pippa

You have about two hours. At the end of it, a program you built will be running inside a real assistant, answering real questions.

No Python to install. No code to write yourself.

What you're building

Pippa is a personal assistant. When someone asks it something, Pippa decides whether one of its installed apps can answer.

An app is a small program that does one job. Pippa pulls the useful bits out of the question, hands them to your app, and your app returns facts. Pippa writes the sentence around them.

What you need

  • Claude Code, installed and signed in.
  • A GitHub account you can push from. You'll have had an invitation to your repository — accept it.
  • A Pippa account, from your Pippa invitation email, and an authenticator app on your phone. Set this up first — see step 4. The address it arrives at goes in your app's manifest.yaml; that is what makes the app yours.

That's the lot. Claude Code does the rest.

  1. The only thing you have to do

    Point Claude Code at your repo

    Open Claude Code and paste your repository link together with what you want built:

    You https://github.com/Pippa-Labs/pippa-hack-yourname

    Here's my repo for a Pippa app. Please clone it and build me an app that tells me when the next high tide is at a UK beach.

    Claude clones it, reads the instructions that ship inside, and starts building. It already knows the rules, the contract and the usual mistakes.

    Keep going in plain language: "add low tide too", "make the error friendlier when the beach isn't found".

    No idea what to build?

    Ask Claude: "what could I build here?" — or open docs/IDEAS.md in your repo, which has a couple of dozen, most with a free public API that needs no sign-up.

  2. Claude does this

    It gets pushed

    When the app is ready, Claude commits and pushes it. That push is the deploy — there is nothing else to run.

    If anything is wrong, the build stops and says exactly what. Tell Claude; it will read the log, fix it and push again.

  3. About 2 minutes

    You get told it's published

    You get an email — a comment on your commit — confirming your app published, with its name and what it can do. There's a green tick in the Actions tab of your repo too, if you'd rather watch.

    Published to you, not to everybody

    A new app is private until a reviewer approves it. You can install it and use it straight away; nobody else can see that it exists. Step 5 is how it becomes public.

    That's why manifest.yaml has your email address in it — owner_email, the address you sign in to Pippa with. It is the only thing that says the app is yours. Get it wrong and the app publishes happily and then you can't see it either, with nothing anywhere reporting an error.

    Know this before you push it: in a public repository anyone can read that address in manifest.yaml, and it stays in the git history even if you change it later.

  4. The moment

    Try it in Pippa

    Do this bit early

    Setting up your Pippa account takes five minutes and needs an authenticator app on your phone. Don't leave it until your app is ready.

    1. Create your account from your Pippa invitation email. The link goes to console.getpippa.ai, not the app itself.
    2. Set up two-factor. Not optional. Save the backup codes — they're shown once.
    3. Open app.getpippa.ai/apps, find your app by name — it's in the list because it's yours — and install it.
    4. Go to the chat and just ask it something — start with one of your own example phrases. Installing it is how you switch it on: from then on, Pippa decides for herself when your app should answer.

    When Pippa answers using your app, the reply carries a small green via <your app name> badge. That's how you know it was yours. It also says who chose it: picked by Pippa, or you picked it.

    Want to ask your app directly?

    Start the message with @ and pick your app from the list. Pippa skips deciding and hands the question straight to your app — the quickest way to check the app itself works. It tells you nothing about whether Pippa would have picked it on her own, though, so ask without the @ too.

    Just published and can't see it?

    The Apps page loads the list once. Reload the page. Still missing? Check that owner_email in manifest.yaml is the address you're signed in as — a private app you don't own is a private app you can't see.

  5. When you're happy with it

    Show it to someone, then submit it

    Show it to someone first. On your app's page in Pippa you can add a tester by email address, and they can install and use your app while it's still private. The person next to you asking it something you never thought of is the most useful ten minutes you'll spend on it.

    Then submit it. Press Submit for review on that same page. A reviewer reads your manifest — the name, the summary, what the app can do, the phrases that trigger it — and either approves it, which makes it public to everybody, or sends it back with a reason you can read.

    Rejected isn't a verdict on you; it's a list of changes. Fix them, push, submit again. You can keep pushing while you wait — it stays in the queue.

    There's no command for any of this and nothing for Claude to run: submitting is a button because it's you saying "this one's ready", which is a different thing from a push.

    Changing an app that's already approved

    Bump the version number in manifest.yaml and push. The new version is private to you again; the approved one keeps working for everyone until the new one is approved in turn. docs/REVIEW_AND_SHARING.md has the detail.

The one thing that decides whether this works

Read this before you pick an idea

Build something that answers with a fact or a figure.

Pippa only reaches for an app when the question needs something looked up or worked out. Apps that mainly rewrite or generate text tend to sit unused, because Pippa answers those herself before any app is consulted. It isn't a bug in your app — it simply never gets asked.

Gets usedUsually doesn't
calculations and conversionstranslating
live lookups — weather, tides, transport, pricessummarising, rewriting
anything about a named placewriting poems, names, copy
anything about a date or a timedefinitions, acronyms
working out numbers someone gives youexplaining how things work

The second thing that matters is the example phrases in your manifest.yaml — they're how Pippa recognises your app. Claude writes them, but if your app isn't being picked, that's almost always why. Ask Claude to add more, written the way a person would actually type.

If something goes wrong

  • Tell Claude. It can read the build log and fix it — the fastest path for nearly everything.
  • docs/TROUBLESHOOTING.md — the errors you're most likely to hit.
  • docs/IDEAS.md — app ideas with APIs that need no key.
  • docs/REVIEW_AND_SHARING.md — who can see your app before it's approved, how to let a friend try it, and how to submit it.
  • Ask an organiser. Bring your repo name, what you asked Pippa, and what it said.
A tip worth taking

Get something deployed in the first twenty minutes, before the app is finished. Ask Claude to set the app id, give it a name, and push the example app as it is. Watch it go live, install it, ask it a question. Then you know the whole pipeline works — so when something breaks later, you know it's your app and not the plumbing.

Nothing is on show while you do it: a published app is private to you until it's reviewed, so an unfinished one going out costs you nothing.

Everything on this page is also in your repository's README.md, which is the copy that stays up to date. Build something you'd actually use.
